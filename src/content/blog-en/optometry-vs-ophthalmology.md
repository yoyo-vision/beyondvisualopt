## Ophthalmologist or Optical Shop for an Eye Exam? Start With What You Need Solved

If something is wrong with your eyes, see an ophthalmologist first: redness, pain, vision that suddenly goes unclear, a jump in floaters, or headaches with no obvious cause all count. If your prescription has simply changed and you want a new pair of glasses, you're over 15 and your eyes feel fine, an optical shop with an optometry clinic is enough. Children 15 and under need to go through an ophthalmologist first; for children under 6, optometry staff may not perform refraction by law.

Ophthalmologists handle diagnosis, treatment, medication and dilation. Optometry staff perform refraction and dispensing under Article 12 of Taiwan's Optometric Personnel Act, and when they find vision that can't be corrected to normal, the law requires them to refer you to a medical institution.

## You've Seen the Ophthalmologist and Been Told "You Can Get New Glasses"

The doctor examines you, says your prescription has changed, and suggests getting a new pair. You now have a slip of paper with numbers on it, and only once you're outside the clinic does the next step occur to you: where do I get the glasses made? And when I hand over this slip, what else will they need to measure?

Underneath, those two questions are the same question. Who may diagnose, who may perform refraction, and for which ages — the law draws a line for each. Once you can see where those lines are, you won't need to guess who to go to next.

## Ophthalmologists and Optometry Staff: Licenses, Scope and NHI in One Table

Here are the two side by side:

| | Ophthalmology clinic | Optometry clinic |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Ophthalmologist (physician's license) | Optometry staff (optometrist or optometric technician license) |
| Core task | Diagnosing and treating eye disease | Measuring refractive status and dispensing glasses |
| What they can do | Diagnosis, medication, dilation, surgery, laser | Refraction, issuing a refraction result report, dispensing |
| Age | Patients of any age | No refraction for children under 6; under an ophthalmologist's guidance for those 15 and under |
| NHI | Outpatient visits covered by NHI | Out of pocket, not covered by NHI |

The table is split by place to keep it readable, but people and places aren't actually tied together. Article 9 of the Optometric Personnel Act lists where optometry staff can practice — medical institutions, optometry clinics and optical companies (shops) — so an ophthalmology clinic can have optometry staff too. The reverse isn't true: nobody at an optometry clinic can treat you for an illness.

### Ophthalmologists: Diagnosis, Treatment, Medication, Dilation

Why your eye is red, why it hurts, why your vision suddenly went unclear, whether you need drops, whether you need surgery — these judgments are medical, and only a physician can make them. Dilation sits on this side too: it starts with drops that relax the eye and enlarge the pupil, and putting in those drops is itself a medical act. After an ophthalmology visit, what you take away is the doctor's judgment and a set of numbers; the glasses themselves usually have to be made somewhere else.

### Optometry Staff: Refraction and Dispensing (Article 12, Optometric Personnel Act)

Article 12 lists what optometry staff may do, item by item. For optometrists, the first item is "non-invasive measurement of the eye's refractive status and related refraction," including refraction done for fitting general contact lenses; it's followed by fitting general contact lenses, teaching the use of low-vision aids, and refraction carried out on a physician's consultation or referral order. For optometric technicians, the scope is refraction for general myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and presbyopia, without the low-vision item. In other words, an ophthalmologist can also write an order asking optometry staff to do your refraction.

What's left once the exam is done? Article 13 requires optometry staff to make a record every time they practice, sign it and date it, and to provide a refraction result report when the person asks for one. The legal term is literally "refraction result report," not "prescription." At Beyond Visual, exam records are kept by the store, and we can issue the report when you need it.

### NHI: Covered at the Ophthalmologist, Out of Pocket at an Optometry Clinic

Ophthalmology outpatient visits are covered by NHI; refraction and glasses at an optometry clinic are paid out of pocket and not covered by NHI. As for what it costs, Article 19 requires optometry clinics to display their practice license and fee schedule somewhere clearly visible, so you'll find them as you walk in. At both Beyond Visual stores, a full eye exam is charged as a separate fee, and the amount is on that posted schedule; if you want to ask before you set out, you can also ask by phone. If you come back for glasses within six months of the exam, the exam fee is included in the cost of your glasses; the separate fee only applies if you have the exam without getting glasses. ## What Optometry Staff May Not Do by Law

Article 12 lists what is allowed, and the limits are written into the same article — plus one more in Article 6 of the Enforcement Rules.

### Under 6: No Refraction

The last sentence of Article 12, Paragraph 1, Item 1 is a proviso: refraction for children under six years of age "shall not be performed." The law says "shall not," which leaves no room for discussion. If a child isn't yet 6, optometry staff may not take on measuring their prescription by law, and the child should be examined by an ophthalmologist.

### Ages 6–15: Refraction Under an Ophthalmologist's Guidance, With the First One After a Physician Rules Out Pseudomyopia

The same item states that refraction for those 15 and under shall be carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance. "15 and under" includes age 15, and contact lens refraction is no exception. Article 6, Paragraph 2 of the Enforcement Rules adds one more step: for children aged 6 to 15, before the first refraction and the first pair of glasses, a physician must confirm the myopia is not pseudomyopia before optometry staff can take over.

So a child under 6 goes straight to an ophthalmologist, because optometry staff may not perform refraction for them by law. For ages 6 to 15 (inclusive), the first refraction and first pair of glasses start with a physician confirming the myopia is not pseudomyopia, and optometry staff cannot, and should not, skip that first step. When our stores perform refraction for a child in this age range, it's done under an ophthalmologist's guidance, in coordination with the physician. Children are covered separately in [My Child's Vision Isn't Good: Ophthalmologist First, or Glasses First?](/en/blog/child-vision-check-where/)

### Dilation, Diagnosis, Medication: Medical Acts

Diagnosis isn't on Article 12's list, and neither is medication. Putting in dilating drops, judging whether an eye is diseased, prescribing medicine and performing surgery are all medical acts, and they belong to ophthalmologists. There's no gray area for optometry staff on these, and there shouldn't be.

## Getting Glasses From an Ophthalmologist's Prescription: What Still Needs Measuring

The main thing on the slip is the prescription: how much myopia or hyperopia, how much astigmatism, what axis, and for people who need help up close, possibly an addition. The prescription decides what the lens has to be. But where on your face the lens has to sit is something the slip can't answer, because when it was written, you hadn't chosen a frame yet.

### Pupillary Distance, Fitting Height, Vertex Distance, Pantoscopic Tilt

[Pupillary distance](/en/blog/pupillary-distance-pd/) is the distance between the centers of your two pupils. Some prescriptions include it; if not, it has to be measured. The lens's optical center has to line up with it, and if it's off, you get a pulling feeling when you look through the lens. Fitting height is where your pupil falls within the frame; it matters most for progressives, because the distance and near zones are positioned by it. [Vertex distance](/en/blog/vertex-distance/) is the distance from the back of the lens to the cornea; the stronger the prescription, the more a few millimeters of difference changes the power your eye actually receives. Pantoscopic tilt is how far the frame tilts forward, which changes where your line of sight passes through the lens when you look down.

Fitting height, vertex distance and pantoscopic tilt can only be measured once you've chosen a frame, put it on and had it adjusted to how you normally wear it. The ophthalmologist's prescription is the starting point; the glasses aren't fully specified until these numbers are measured.

## When Vision Can't Be Corrected: What Optometry Staff Do

Trial lenses go in one after another, but the reading on the chart stops at a certain line and won't improve however the power is adjusted. Article 12, Paragraph 3 is blunt about it: if optometry staff find in the course of their work that vision cannot be corrected to normal, they shall refer the person to a medical institution for diagnosis and treatment. Article 6, Paragraph 3 of the Enforcement Rules adds that the referral must be made on a referral form.

At that moment there's very little optometry staff can do, and that is exactly where the boundary of this job falls. We can measure that your vision won't correct; we can't measure why — and saying why is a diagnosis. So we don't guess at what it might be, and we won't tell you "it's probably nothing." If something abnormal turns up during the exam (for example, vision that can't be corrected to normal, or signs of a possible eye disease), the optometry staff will recommend a referral to an ophthalmologist for further examination, and write down the prescription and vision they measured so the doctor has one more piece of information. Honestly, at that point a referral form does you more good than a pair of glasses made anyway.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I need to see an ophthalmologist before going to an optometry clinic?**

If you're over 15, you simply want glasses, and your eyes aren't red, painful or suddenly unclear, you can go straight to an optometry clinic. For those 15 and under, the law sets an order: optometry staff may not perform refraction for children under 6; for ages 6 to 15, before the first refraction and the first pair of glasses, a physician must confirm the myopia is not pseudomyopia, and all refraction in this age group is carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance. For when adults are better off seeing a doctor first, see [Do You Need to See an Ophthalmologist Before Getting Glasses?](/en/blog/eye-exam-before-glasses/)

**Q: Can an optometry clinic give eye drops or do medical tests?**

No. Eye drops and dilation are medical acts that need an ophthalmologist's judgment and prescription, so please see an ophthalmologist. The same goes for tests such as eye pressure and visual fields: our stores don't have these instruments, and they need to be done at an ophthalmology clinic.

**Q: Can an ordinary optical shop do eye exams?**

It depends on whether the shop has an optometry clinic set up inside. Under Article 4 of the Enforcement Rules of the Optometric Personnel Act, an optical company (shop) must set up an optometry clinic within its premises before it can carry out refraction, and refraction is performed by optometry staff holding a practice license. An optical shop without an optometry clinic can sell frames, make glasses from a refraction result, and adjust frames, but by law it may not carry out refraction. When you walk in, the optometry clinic's practice license is displayed somewhere clearly visible, as the law requires. You can also check by name whether the person examining you is registered to practice, on the Ministry of Health and Welfare's medical personnel lookup system.

**Q: Is an eye exam more accurate at an optical shop or at an ophthalmologist's?**

Accuracy depends on the person doing the exam and how complete the process is; whether it happens at an ophthalmology clinic or at an optical shop with an optometry clinic doesn't decide that by itself. Measurements that have to be taken after dilating drops are medical and need to be done at an ophthalmology clinic. At both Beyond Visual stores, a full clinical eye exam takes about 45–60 minutes, and besides the prescription, every exam includes a binocular vision assessment.
