## "Contacts or glasses, which is actually better for my eyes?"

We hear this a lot in the trial-fitting chair. The people asking usually own both: contacts for work, glasses at home. They want to know which one should be their main pair.

Both correct the same prescription. What differs is where the lens sits. Glasses sit about twelve millimetres in front of the eye, and contact lenses sit directly on the cornea. That gap changes how big things look, how much you can see, and how hard your eyes work up close. A lens worn on the eye also brings a few things glasses never have to deal with. So which is better depends on which of these matters most to you.

---

## How contact lenses and glasses differ optically

### Image size: myopic glasses shrink things, contacts barely do

A lens for short-sightedness is a minus (concave) lens. When it sits some distance from the eye, it makes what you see slightly smaller, and the higher the prescription, the more it shrinks. A contact lens sits on the cornea with almost no gap, so the shrinking effect is much smaller. That's why contact lens wearers often find things look smaller and farther away when they switch back to glasses, and why people with high myopia notice it most. The glasses aren't wrong; the lens is just in a different place. What else to watch for above -6.00 D is covered in [The Complete Guide to High-Myopia Eyewear](/en/guide/high-myopia/).

### Field of view: the ring the frame blocks

Your view through glasses is bounded by the frame. When your gaze moves toward the edge of the lens, people with higher prescriptions also notice the edge is a little blurrier than the centre and straight lines bend slightly. A contact lens moves with your eye, so wherever you look you're looking through the centre of the lens, with no frame in the way. The difference shows most during sport, or when you glance sideways while driving.

### Power: contacts need their own prescription

The same eye needs a different power depending on where the lens sits. For short-sighted people, the contact lens power is usually a little weaker than the glasses power; for long-sighted people it's the other way round, and the contact lens needs to be stronger. From about ±4.00 D the gap is large enough that it has to be converted. The numbers for each step are in [What Is Vertex Distance?](/en/blog/vertex-distance/). At low powers the numbers barely differ, but the astigmatism power and axis, and the lens curvature, still have to be confirmed for the contact lens.

### Astigmatism: soft lenses rely on positioning, and high astigmatism can be compared with RGP

In glasses, the astigmatism correction is ground into the lens relative to the frame's horizontal line, so as long as the frame sits straight, the axis stays put. A soft toric contact lens relies on its own design to settle in a fixed orientation. Blinking, rubbing your eyes, or the lens drying out can rotate it slightly, and when the axis drifts the image wobbles, more so the higher the astigmatism. People with high astigmatism have one more option, rigid RGP lenses; how they compare is covered in [The Complete Guide to RGP Rigid Contact Lenses](/en/guide/rgp-contact-lens/).

## Worn on the eye: three more things to manage, oxygen, infection, and dryness

Glasses rest on your nose and never touch the surface of your eye. A contact lens is a medical device that covers the cornea all day, which adds three things to manage.

The first is oxygen. The cornea has no blood vessels and gets its oxygen mainly from the air through the tear film. With a lens on top, how much gets through depends on the material and how long you wear it. Wearing lenses too long, or sleeping in them, leaves the cornea short of oxygen over time. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's EyeWiki entry "Contact Lens Complications" notes that hypoxia and inflammation can cause new blood vessels to grow into the edge of the cornea. That needs to be checked by an ophthalmologist.

The second is infection. The same source lists contact lens wear as a primary risk factor for corneal infection (microbial keratitis), and the risk rises with sleeping in lenses, overwearing them, poor hygiene, and not using the right solution. Glasses carry no such risk.

The third is dryness. Soft lenses contain water, and in air-conditioning, or when you blink less at a screen, the surface of your eye dries out more easily. Dryness doesn't only come from the lens, and the condition of your tear film needs to be checked by an ophthalmologist; our stores don't do that check. Five common reasons contacts feel dry and tiring by the afternoon are covered in [Contact lenses dry and tiring by afternoon?](/en/blog/contact-lens-fatigue/).

How many hours a day you can wear lenses differs between soft and rigid lenses, and follows what our optometry staff tell you and the product instructions.

## When we suggest relying mainly on glasses

Some people are better off wearing glasses most of the time, whatever type of contact lens they use, and keeping contacts for particular occasions.

If your eyes are often dry, or itch as soon as allergy season starts, a lens covering the eye makes the discomfort more noticeable, and persistent dryness should be checked by an ophthalmologist first. If you spend most of the day at a screen in air-conditioning, you already blink less, and wearing contacts all day on top of that adds to the load. If you have diabetes or a weaker immune system, a corneal infection is harder to deal with, so ask your doctor before wearing contacts. And if your schedule is irregular, you often work late, or you can't remember when you opened your lenses, you're the most likely to overwear them or sleep in them.

There are age rules too. Under Article 12 of Taiwan's Optometrists Act, contact lens refraction for those aged fifteen and under must be carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance, and is not permitted for children under six. We do not currently fit contact lenses for children aged fifteen and under, so please have an ophthalmologist assess your child first; how each age group is handled is in [The Complete Guide to Children's Myopia Tracking](/en/guide/children-myopia/).

## If you use both, how to split your time

Most contact lens wearers also have a pair of glasses. The question is how to divide the days.

A common pattern is glasses for the commute and the office, and contacts for sport, social events, or photos. If you only wear lenses once or twice a week, daily disposables are the simplest: you open only what you use. If you wear lenses almost every day, a two-week or monthly schedule can make sense, as long as you keep to the replacement date and the cleaning.

If you wear contacts every day, don't neglect the prescription in your glasses either. You rely on them in the evening when you take your lenses out, on the days your eyes are red and you need to stop wearing contacts, and when you've left your solution at home. Keep both prescriptions accurate, and switching between them won't leave you clear in one and blurry in the other.

## Short-sighted in contacts, near vision gets harder sooner after forty than in glasses

We said the difference between contacts and glasses comes down to where the lens sits. That distance has one more consequence at near, and few people notice it.

When a minus lens sits some distance in front of the eye, your eye has to accommodate (focus) a little less to see something close. Put the lens on the cornea and that small saving disappears. Run the numbers with the vertex formula Fc = F ÷ (1 − dF): for a -6.00 D myope with the lens 12 mm from the cornea, reading a phone 40 cm away takes about 2.2 D of accommodation in glasses and 2.5 D in contacts, roughly 0.3 D more. The higher the prescription, the bigger the gap; at -3.00 D it's about 0.17 D more.

When you're young, accommodation is plentiful and you won't notice. Around forty it starts to decline, and the same person in contacts will find they need to hold the phone farther away or enlarge the text a little earlier than they would in glasses. When people say "my glasses are fine, but in contacts I can't read my phone," this is usually why. The contact lens prescription isn't wrong.

At that stage there are a few options: a pair of reading glasses over your contacts for close work, multifocal contact lenses, or switching back to glasses during the hours you do a lot of close work. ## FAQ

**Q: If I already have a glasses prescription, do I still need a separate contact lens fitting?**

Strictly speaking, yes. Contact lens power has to be measured separately, and if you have astigmatism the cylinder power and axis need confirming again. Most regular soft lenses are trial-fitted first to check your vision; we don't keep trial lenses for soft toric or multifocal contacts, so our optometry staff assess those before deciding. Buying contacts straight from your glasses prescription can leave you with a prescription that's slightly off.

**Q: My eyes feel dry when I wear contacts. What can I do?**

Take the lenses out first and rest your eyes, and think back on whether you've worn them longer than usual, or spent more time in air-conditioning or at a screen these past few days. If your eyes are still dry after resting, or you have redness, pain, light sensitivity, or a sudden drop in vision, stop wearing them and see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible; checks of the tear film and eye surface are done by ophthalmologists. If it's just dryness by the afternoon, the common causes and what you can adjust first are in [Contact lenses dry and tiring by afternoon?](/en/blog/contact-lens-fatigue/).

**Q: For sport, should I wear contacts or sports glasses?**

It depends on the sport. For running or ball games, where you sweat and turn your head, contacts give you a view with no frame in the way and won't slide down your nose. For swimming, surfing, or anything in the water, don't wear contacts: water isn't sterile. For contact sports, an extra layer in front of your eyes is reassuring, so consider prescription sports glasses, or a sports strap on your regular glasses. We stock sports frames and sports straps, fitted to your glasses prescription.
