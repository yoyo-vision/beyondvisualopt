## Two Kinds of Raindrops at a Red Light

You stop at a red light on your scooter in the rain. On one pair of glasses the raindrops gather into separate beads that fly off with a shake of your head; on another they spread into a single sheet, and the traffic lights ahead blur together. Both pairs have the same prescription. The difference is the outermost layer of the lens.

A coating is a stack of thin films on the lens surface, measured in nanometers, and each layer has its own job: some reduce reflections, some resist wear, some keep water droplets and oil from clinging. Same prescription, same refractive index: what changes how your glasses feel day to day is which layers are stacked on the surface.

## 6 Common Lens Coatings

### Anti-Reflective: Fewer Reflections and Ghost Images

A lens without anti-reflective coating reflects part of the light off its surface. In photos the lenses show up as a white glare, and when you drive at night into oncoming headlights, rings of ghost images appear on the lens. Anti-reflective coating uses thin-film interference to cancel out the reflected light, reducing reflections and glare and giving a clearer, higher-contrast image. For the full explanation of how it "cancels" reflections, see [How Anti-Reflective Coating Works: Why a Thin Film Stops Glare](/en/blog/anti-reflective-coating-principle/).

### Hard Coating: Scratch Resistance

Today's mainstream resin lenses are fairly soft. A hard coating adds a tougher film to the surface so the lens resists wear better, with fewer of the fine scratches left by everyday wiping and setting it down. Scratch-resistant doesn't mean scratch-proof: wiping with your shirt hem dry or laying your glasses lens-down on a table will still damage them.

### Water- and Oil-Repellent: Easy to Wipe Clean

The water- and oil-repellent layer is usually the outermost layer, making the lens surface shed water and oil: water beads up and rolls off, and fingerprints and sweat wipe off more easily. Without this layer, one swipe of a fingerprint smears across the lens, and the more you wipe, the worse it looks.

### Anti-Fog: Masks and Air-Conditioned Rooms

Talking in a mask, stepping out of an air-conditioned room, leaning over a hot bowl of noodle soup, and your lenses turn white in an instant. Anti-fog treatment changes the properties of the lens surface so condensation spreads into a clear, even film of water instead of tiny droplets that block your view. Neither of our two stores currently offers anti-fog lenses. If you need this, mention it during your eye exam and we'll talk through other options with you.

### Blue-Light Filtering: Filters Part of Short-Wavelength Blue Light

A blue-light filtering coating filters out part of the shorter-wavelength blue light in the visible spectrum. A common approach is a surface coating that reflects this band back, which is why these lenses often show a slight blue-violet reflection when you look at them from the side. It's an optical treatment. In Taiwan, prescription eyeglass lenses are regulated as medical devices, and a coating cannot claim to prevent or treat any eye disease. Which wavelengths count as blue light, and how absorptive and reflective types differ, is covered in [What Does a Blue Light Filter Lens Actually Filter?](/en/blog/blue-light-filter-what/)

### UV400: Clear Lenses Can Block UV Too

UV400 means blocking ultraviolet light at wavelengths below 400 nanometers, and it can be built into the lens material or the coating. A clear lens can have UV400 too. It has nothing to do with how dark the lens is; you don't need photochromic lenses or sunglasses to block UV.

## Lens Coating Comparison Table

| Coating | What it deals with | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-reflective | Reflected light and ghost images on the lens surface | Multiple layers, stacked on the hard coating |
| Hard coating | Wear resistance | The layer right against the lens material |
| Water- and oil-repellent | Water spots, fingerprints, and grease sticking | Outermost layer |
| Anti-fog | Fogging caused by temperature changes | Surface treatment |
| Blue-light filtering | Part of short-wavelength blue light | Reflected by the coating or absorbed by the material |
| UV400 | Ultraviolet light below 400 nanometers | Material or coating |

> Each manufacturer names its coatings differently and uses different numbers of layers and processes. For actual specifications, refer to the manufacturer's labeling and documentation.

## Choosing Coatings by Your Daily Habits

Not everyone needs every coating piled on. Before you choose, think about what your day looks like:

### You Drive at Night Often

Oncoming headlights, streetlights, traffic signals: at night your lenses face a whole row of high-contrast light sources. The anti-reflective layer is the one that matters most to you. Fewer reflections mean fainter ghost rings around headlights. For what else to watch for when driving at night, read [Oncoming Headlights Glaring at Night?](/en/blog/driving-lens-night-glare/)

### You Look at Screens All Day

People who sit in front of a screen all day often ask whether to add blue-light filtering. What this layer does is filter out part of the short-wavelength blue light. Whether to add it depends on how long and how you use your eyes, and you can look at sample lenses first to see whether the tint and reflection are acceptable to you. For how to adjust your screen habits, see our [guide to tired eyes from screens](/en/guide/digital-eye-strain/).

### You Wear a Mask Often or Move In and Out of Air Conditioning

Fogging comes from temperature differences. Pressing the top edge of your mask down so your breath doesn't rise up cuts out most of it. Neither of our stores currently offers anti-fog lenses; tell us about your working environment during your eye exam, and we can adjust the frame shape and how it sits on your face too.

### Your Lenses Always End Up Scratched

Look at how you clean them first, then at the coating. The water- and oil-repellent layer makes fingerprints and grease easy to wipe off, and the hard coating makes the surface a bit more wear-resistant; but if you're in the habit of dry-wiping with your shirt hem, even the best coating won't last long.

## Coatings Age, and How You Use Them Matters More Than How Many Layers

A coating is a surface process with a limited lifespan. More layers or a higher tier doesn't mean it lasts longer. When we see cracking, peeling, or haze that won't wipe clean at the store, it usually traces back to the same few habits.

At the top of the list is dry-wiping: wiping with a cloth without rinsing first turns the grit on the surface into sandpaper for your lenses. Next is heat. Leave your glasses on the car dashboard in summer, or wear them into a sauna or hot spring, and the coating and the lens underneath expand and contract by different amounts until, over time, the coating cracks. Then there are chemicals: alcohol and strongly acidic or alkaline cleaners all damage coatings.

So before you choose coatings, think about whether you'll actually drop these habits. If you won't, an extra layer won't last long. If you do, the basic layers will serve you for a long time.

## FAQ

**Q: Is a blue-light coating necessary?**

It depends on how you use your eyes. A blue-light filtering coating filters out part of the short-wavelength blue light; it's an optical treatment. In Taiwan, prescription eyeglass lenses are regulated as medical devices, and a coating cannot claim to prevent or treat eye disease. If you spend long hours on screens, it's worth considering; if your screen time is short, you may not need it. Before adding it, make sure you're comfortable with the tint and reflection. For the principle, see [What Does a Blue Light Filter Lens Actually Filter?](/en/blog/blue-light-filter-what/); for screen habits, see [Screens Look Blurry by Mid-Afternoon](/en/blog/digital-eye-strain-solution/).

**Q: Do anti-reflective and smudge-resistant coatings have to go together?**

It's not a hard rule, but they usually come as a pair. Anti-reflective coating deals with reflections; the water- and oil-repellent layer deals with how easily the lens wipes clean. An anti-reflective lens is so transparent that fingerprints and grease on it actually stand out more, so most anti-reflective lenses come from the factory with a water- and oil-repellent layer on the outside. The combinations actually available depend on the lens series.

**Q: What's the difference between multi-layer and single-layer coatings?**

The difference is how thoroughly reflections are cancelled. A single layer cancels reflections cleanly for only one band of color, so other colors still reflect; a multi-layer coating stacks several films of different thicknesses, each handling one band of wavelengths, so reflections across the whole visible range are pushed down. Hard coating and water- and oil-repellent layers are separate functional layers on top. More layers isn't automatically better. What matters is whether each layer is one you'll actually use; a function you don't need is just one more layer you're paying for.
