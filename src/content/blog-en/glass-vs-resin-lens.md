## "My Dad's Old Glasses Have Gone Twenty Years Without a Scratch"

This conversation comes up a few times a year at the shop. An older customer sets a pair of glasses with some age on them down on the counter — the lenses still clear, barely a scratch to be found — and asks: "Why do lenses these days get scratched up in just a few months? The old kind was better, wasn't it?"

Pick it up and give it a heft check, and the answer is right there in your hand: that pair is unbelievably heavy. Those are **glass lenses**.

Today, more than 90% of what we fit in the shop is **resin lenses**. What happened in between can't be waved away with "the old stuff was just better made." Glass still wins in some places today, and it's lost so badly in others that it's nearly left the market. This article lays the two materials side by side and, at the end, helps you figure out: **given your prescription, your frame, and what you actually look at every day, which side you should be on.**

---

## Bottom Line First: They Win in Different Places, But One Loss Is Fatal

Two things matter most; everything else is detail:

- **Scratch resistance: glass wins, and wins by a lot.**
- **Impact safety when dropped: resin is far safer. This is the main reason glass has nearly exited the Taiwan market.**

You'll wear a pair of glasses for years. Getting scratched is annoying; shattering is dangerous — those two things don't carry equal weight, and the market eventually tipped toward safety.

## Where the Two Materials Actually Differ

### 1. Density and Weight: Glass Really Is Heavier

Density is where the two materials diverge the most.

- Standard resin lens material (CR-39) has a density of roughly **1.32 g/cm³**.
- Traditional crown glass runs roughly **2.5 g/cm³**. High-index glass (1.80, 1.90, and up) that uses rare-earth elements to stay thin pushes that density even higher.

Translated to everyday terms: **for the same prescription in the same frame, a glass lens weighs roughly twice as much as a resin one.**

Who feels this most?

- **People with high myopia**: the lenses are already thick and heavy, and doubling that weight — your nose bridge will tell you the honest answer by the end of the day. (Related: [Complete Guide to High Myopia Glasses](/en/guide/high-myopia/))
- **Progressive lens wearers**: extra weight drags the frame down, and once the frame slips, the lens's optical center stops lining up with your pupil. For progressives, that means the whole distance/intermediate/near zone layout shifts upward — you tilt your head back further to see the screen and drop your gaze further to see the ground. **Progressives are the design that can least afford a slipping frame.**
- **Presbyopia patients**: a pair of glasses worn from morning to night puts cumulative load on your nose and ears. Lighter isn't a luxury here — it's the difference between making it through the whole day comfortably or not.

### 2. Refractive Index and Thickness: Glass Can Get Thinner Than Resin Can

Refractive index determines how thick a lens needs to be for a given prescription — the higher the number, the thinner the lens, and that logic is the same for both materials. (New to this term? See: [What Is Lens "Refractive Index"? The Difference Between 1.60, 1.67, and 1.74](/en/blog/lens-refractive-index/))

The difference is in the ceiling:

- **Resin**: mainstream options run from 1.50, 1.56, 1.60, 1.67, up to **1.74**.
- **Glass**: common specs go all the way up to **1.80, 1.90**.

So at very high prescriptions, glass can still squeeze out a bit more thinness — that's the technical reason it hasn't disappeared entirely. (Among the brands we carry, ZEISS single-vision glass lenses come in 1.50 / 1.60 / 1.70 / 1.80 / 1.90 — full specs are on our [lens page](https://beyondvisualopt.com/en/lenses/).)

But there's a real trade-off to account for here: **getting glass thinner doesn't make it lighter.** High-index glass has an even higher density, so you're trading "heavier material" for "thinner edge." Whether that trade is worth it depends on whether you care more about visible edge thickness or the actual weight on your nose bridge. There's no universal answer here — you have to try it with your actual prescription.

### 3. Abbe Number and Dispersion: Glass Really Does Have Cleaner Optics

Abbe number reflects a lens's **dispersion** — how white light splits apart and shows up as a faint colored fringe around object edges, streetlights, or high-contrast boundaries. The higher the Abbe number, the less dispersion and the cleaner the edges.

Rough comparison (actual values vary by manufacturer formula):

| Material | Refractive Index | Abbe Number (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Resin CR-39 | 1.50 | ~58 |
| Resin | 1.67 | ~32 |
| Resin | 1.74 | ~33 |
| Glass (crown) | 1.52 | ~58–59 |
| Glass | 1.70 | ~39 |
| Glass | 1.80 | ~35 |
| Glass | 1.90 | ~30 |

The key comparison is **at the same thinness level**: a 1.70 glass lens has an Abbe number around 39, while a 1.67 resin lens sits around 32. **To get to a similarly thin lens, glass typically shows less dispersion than resin.**

So when do you actually notice it? Not while reading in daylight — it shows up at night, behind the wheel. Oncoming headlights, traffic signals, backlit signs — these high-contrast light sources are exactly where dispersion becomes visible. The higher your prescription and the further your line of sight drifts from the lens's optical center (like glancing at a rearview mirror), the more noticeable that colored fringe gets. So the people who insist "glass is just clearer" are usually people with high prescriptions who drive a lot at night. What they're feeling is real — it's just that the "clearer" they're describing is about dispersion, not visual acuity.

One more variable worth mentioning: how clean the optics look also depends on lens surface design, which is a separate topic — see [Aspheric vs. Spherical Lenses](/en/blog/aspheric-vs-spherical-lens/).

### 4. Scratch Resistance: Glass Wins, No Contest

This is glass's most solid advantage — no ambiguity here.

Glass has a hard surface, so everyday dust, fabric, or desk contact rarely leaves a mark. Resin, by contrast, starts out as a naturally soft material, which is why every resin lens today ships with a hard coating to compensate for that weakness. Coated resin lenses today are far more durable than they were twenty years ago, but in raw hardness, they still can't match glass.

So that customer's claim — "twenty years and not a scratch" — is true. That's not nostalgia talking. That's material physics.

### 5. Impact Resistance: Resin Wins, and This One Decided the Market

Here's the one that matters most.

Glass is hard, but hard and tough are two different things. When glass takes an impact, it shatters — with sharp edges. Resin, on impact, deforms, cracks, or breaks apart in a comparatively gentler way. Materials like polycarbonate (PC) and Trivex go a step further in impact resistance, which is why sports lenses, kids' lenses, and workplace eye-protection needs are almost universally made from these materials.

To be clear, no lens can promise it won't break in every accident — that's not a claim we'll make. But the difference in how these materials fail under impact is objectively real, and international standards for lens impact resistance are exactly what pushed glass out of the mainstream.

Back to daily life:

- Biking, commuting, carrying kids, sports, construction or factory sites, the gym — anywhere your glasses might get knocked or dropped onto your face — resin is the more sensible choice.
- Older adults have one more thing to factor in: the odds of dropping your glasses go up with age, and how that drop onto a tile floor ends is very different between the two materials.

### 6. Coating, Tinting, Photochromic: A Big Gap in What's Possible

A large part of a modern lens's value isn't in the base material — it's in **the layers applied on top.** Resin has a clear edge in flexibility here.

| Process | Glass | Resin |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-reflective coating | Possible (vacuum coating technology actually started with glass) | Possible, and can be layered with hard coating, water-repellent, and oil-repellent treatments |
| Tinting (solid / gradient) | Limited — usually relies on the material itself or a surface layer; gradients are difficult | Can be dip-dyed into nearly any color or gradient depth |
| Photochromic | Traditionally done by dispersing silver halide microcrystals evenly through the glass, so **color depth varies with lens thickness** — high prescriptions with a big center-to-edge thickness difference can end up with uneven tinting | Photochromic molecules sit in a surface or substrate layer, so color depth stays relatively even across the same lens |
| Rimless (drilled) frames | Not really recommended — drill points become stress concentration spots | The mainstream choice; PC/Trivex are especially common for rimless |
| Progressive lenses | Almost no current mass-production options on the market | The primary battleground for free-form manufacturing |

The last two rows in this table are the deciding ones.

**If you want rimless or semi-rimless frames**, glass is essentially out. (Related: [Complete Guide to Lens Coatings](/en/blog/lens-coating-guide/), [How Photochromic Lenses Work](/en/blog/photochromic-lens-principle/))

And progressives are really the final answer. Today's high-end progressive designs rely on free-form manufacturing — personalized parameters calculated and cut directly into the back surface of the lens. That entire process and supply chain is built around resin, and glass versions have essentially vanished from the market. So for people with presbyopia, the question "should I choose glass?" is often already answered the moment they decide to go with progressives. (Related: [Complete Guide to Progressive Lenses](/en/guide/progressive-lens/))

## Why Over 90% of What's Fitted in Taiwan Is Resin

It's not that optical shops don't want to sell glass — the entire supply chain moved toward resin:

1. **Safety concerns drove the market**: the gap in impact resistance pushed international markets toward resin starting in the 1970s, and the supply chain followed.
2. **Progressive lenses became mainstream**: an aging population made progressives the dominant product, and progressive free-form manufacturing is built on resin.
3. **Equipment generations shifted**: today's edging machines, drilling equipment, and coating processes in most shops are designed for resin. Grinding and cutting glass is a separate craft that fewer and fewer places can still handle.
4. **Frame design trends**: rimless, semi-rimless, and ultra-thin titanium frames became widespread, and these frame styles rule out glass by design.
5. **Resin caught up on its own**: hard coatings, multi-layer anti-reflective coatings, water- and oil-repellent layers have closed most of resin's biggest weakness. Today's resin lenses are nothing like the kind that used to fog up after three months, twenty years ago.

So the answer isn't really "the old stuff was better made." What actually changed is **the weighting on the scorecard**. Once the market decided to put safety and weight first, glass went from mainstream to a niche option for specific needs.

## So Who Still Genuinely Benefits from Glass Today?

There are cases — the conditions are just specific. These situations are worth bringing glass back into the conversation.

**Your prescription is extremely high, and thickness matters more to you than anything else.** When the edge thickness of a 1.74 resin lens is still more than you can accept, 1.80/1.90 glass is one of the few remaining options with something to offer. The trade-off is you need to accept the weight, and the frame has to be chosen right: small, high-wrap frames with well-designed nose pad support are what it takes to carry that weight.

**Your visual environment is static and clean, and you're extremely sensitive to scratches.** If you work long hours in a fixed indoor spot, rarely take your glasses off, face low impact risk, and are particularly sensitive to surface wear, glass's scratch resistance really will keep your glasses looking new for years.

**You have a high prescription, drive frequently at night, and are especially sensitive to dispersion.** As covered above, this is where glass's Abbe number advantage is most noticeable. One caveat: night-vision comfort isn't just about material — lens design and anti-reflective coating matter just as much. Before switching materials, it's worth confirming those two things are already dialed in.

Outside these three cases, resin is almost always the more sensible answer. In particular, we'd tell these groups directly not to bother considering glass:

- **Office workers staring at screens 8–12 hours a day**: what you need is a pair of glasses that can stay on your face all day without bothering you — weight and frame stability directly determine whether you're still comfortable by the afternoon. Screen-heavy users often also want anti-reflective coating and blue-light filtering, and both integrate more maturely with resin. (Related: [Complete Guide to Digital Eye Strain](/en/guide/digital-eye-strain/))
- **Presbyopia patients wearing progressives**: as covered above, glass options are essentially nonexistent, and frame slippage from the extra weight directly disrupts progressive zone alignment.
- **Commuters, anyone active in sports, parents of young kids, or anyone in a workplace with impact risk**: this one doesn't need explaining.
- **Anyone wanting rimless, semi-rimless, photochromic, or gradient tinting**: the material itself doesn't support it.

## FAQ

**Q: Does anyone still use glass lenses today?**

Yes, but the share is extremely small, and in Taiwan it's usually a special-order item. The people who still use glass generally fall into three groups: very high prescriptions where thickness matters more than even a 1.74 resin lens can satisfy; static, low-impact visual environments with extreme sensitivity to scratches; and a small number of cases needing specific optical specs. Special orders usually take longer to complete. For everyday glasses, progressives, rimless frames, or photochromic/tinted needs, the market is almost entirely resin. Whether it's right for you should be assessed by an optometrist who has reviewed your prescription and frame.

**Q: Do resin lenses scratch easily?**

The raw resin material is indeed softer than glass, but every resin lens today ships with a hard coating, and scratch resistance is far better than older products — under normal use, it should last the full lifespan of a pair of glasses. What actually scratches lenses is usually cleaning habits: wiping dry, using your shirt, or setting them face-down on a table are the three most damaging things you can do. The right way is to rinse off surface dust with water first, then wipe with a clean, dedicated lens cloth. If your environment is unusually dusty, you can discuss coating options with your optometrist when getting fitted.

**Q: Are glass lenses actually clearer?**

Depends what you mean by "clearer." Visual clarity depends on whether your prescription is accurate, whether the lens's optical center is aligned with your pupil, and whether the frame is properly adjusted — none of which relates directly to material. Glass's real optical advantage is **less dispersion**: at the same thinness level, glass typically has a higher Abbe number, so under high-contrast light sources like headlights or streetlights at night, there's less colored fringing around object edges. This is more noticeable for people with high prescriptions who drive often at night; for everyday use, the difference is fairly limited.

**Q: Can glass lenses be made photochromic or with blue-light filtering?**

Anti-reflective coating, yes — glass is actually where coating technology started. For photochromic, there's a catch: traditional glass photochromic lenses disperse light-sensitive silver halide microcrystals evenly through the entire lens, so color depth varies with lens thickness. High prescriptions with a big center-to-edge thickness difference tend to tint unevenly. Tinting and gradient tinting flexibility also fall well short of resin. If you want photochromic, tinting, or blue-light filtering, resin is significantly more capable and mature.

**Q: Will glass be thinner for high myopia?**

Somewhat, since glass can reach a refractive index of 1.80 or 1.90, while resin tops out around 1.74 in the mainstream. But there's another cost to factor in: high-index glass has a higher density, so the thinness you gain comes at the cost of a heavier lens pressing on your nose bridge, and Abbe number also drops at higher indices for glass. In practice, frame size matters too — the bigger the frame and lens diameter, the more the thickness and weight penalty shows up. So for high prescriptions trying to minimize thickness, material is only one variable; frame size choice often matters more. We'd suggest bringing your current glasses in so an optometrist can compare directly before deciding.

## Material Is a Choice; Accurate Fitting Is the Prerequisite

Whichever side you land on, there's an old saying that fits perfectly here: **no material, however good, can perform if the measurements are off or the fit is wrong.**

Glass's low dispersion can't rescue a misaligned optical center. Resin's light weight can't make up for a poorly adjusted frame. The order that determines how comfortable your glasses actually feel is always: "refraction accuracy → fitting parameters → frame adjustment → lens material and design." Material comes last on that list — not because it doesn't matter, but because it only pays off once the first three are already right. (Related: [A Good Optometrist Matters More Than a Good Lens](/en/guide/why-optometrist-matters/))

So if you're on the fence about material, the most direct way to decide is exactly what that customer did: pick up both kinds of lenses and feel the weight difference in your hand. No spec sheet will ever give you that feeling.
