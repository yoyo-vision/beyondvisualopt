## "But I clean them every day"

A customer who wears RGP lenses came in for a follow-up visit and slid his lens case across the counter. The solution inside was three days old. "But I clean them every day," he said. He rubbed the lenses every day; he just hadn't been replacing the solution.

Whether you can keep wearing RGP lenses is half decided on the day of the fitting. The other half is decided every morning and night after that. This piece covers the steps people miss most, and why they can't be skipped. If the instructions for your product, or what our optometry staff showed you in person, differ from what's here, follow those.

---

## A day with RGP lenses, from putting them in to taking them out

### Morning: check your hands, the lenses, and your eyes

Wash your hands with soap, rinse well, and dry them on a lint-free towel or tissue; leftover water and lint on your hands are the most common source of lens contamination. Then hold each lens up to the light. If you see a chip, a crack, or haze that won't wash off, don't wear it, and bring it in for us to check. Finally, take a look at your own eyes: if they're red, itchy, painful, or there's more discharge than usual, give them a day off. If you wear makeup, put your lenses in first and then apply makeup; when removing it, take the lenses out first.

### During the day: three non-negotiables

**Never sleep in daily-wear RGP lenses** (avoid naps in them too), **never let the lenses touch tap water** (tap water is not sterile), and **don't wear them swimming, in hot springs, or in a sauna**.

For everything else, one rule: take the lens out first, don't put up with it. If wind blows grit into your eye while riding a scooter, the worst thing you can do is rub it. Take the lens out, rinse it, put it back in, and wear wind protection for your commute from then on. If your eyes feel dry after a long stretch in air-conditioning, you can use rewetting drops made for rigid lenses; which bottle and how to use it, follow the instructions. Some foreign-body sensation at first is normal: RGP lenses are small and hold their shape, so your eyes need time to adapt. Increase your wearing time gradually on the schedule our optometry staff set for you.

## The three steps people miss most

When you take the lenses out at night, the day isn't over. The daily cleaning is four fixed steps: put the lens in your palm with rigid-lens solution, rub gently in one direction with your fingertip for 20–30 seconds, rinse according to the instructions, and soak it in fresh solution in the case for the time the instructions require. Even when a product says rubbing isn't needed, rubbing is still widely recommended for rigid lenses. Most people remember these four. What they miss is usually one of the three below.

### Replace the solution every day, never top it up

However well you've rubbed the lens, putting it back into yesterday's solution means soaking it in used liquid. After a night of soaking, part of the solution's disinfecting capacity is used up, and what you rubbed off the day before is still in there. So pour it out and refill it every day; don't reuse it, and don't "top up" old solution with new. This is the step we see skipped most often in store.

### Empty and air-dry the case every day, and replace it regularly

The case is the most easily forgotten source of contamination in the whole routine. Empty it every day, rinse it with solution, and let it air-dry face down; don't rinse it with tap water or wipe it with tissue. Over time a case picks up scratches and an invisible film. We suggest replacing it about every 3 months, or as your product instructions say.

### Do protein removal on schedule

Proteins from your tears build up on the lens day by day, and daily rubbing doesn't remove all of them. If your lenses are starting to look hazy or feel scratchier, it's often because protein removal has slipped. For most products it's once a week; follow the instructions on the bottle you use.

## Four signs to stop wearing them and see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible

Redness or pain that doesn't improve after rest; light sensitivity or tearing that won't stop; vision that suddenly becomes blurry; discharge. If you notice any one of these, take the lens out and don't put it back in, and see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible. Bring the lenses, the case, and your solution, since they help the doctor make a judgment. How urgent each symptom is is covered in [Eye Doctor or Optometry Clinic? The Symptom Triage Guide](/en/guide/eye-doctor-or-optometrist/).

Keep a pair of glasses with an adequate prescription at home, so you have something to switch to on days you can't wear your lenses. Never share your lenses or case with anyone, not even for a quick "try."

## Lenses you clean every day still get scratched and warped over time

Every step above deals with dirt on the surface of the lens. The lens itself ages, and cleaning can't do anything about that.

RGP material generally lasts longer than soft lenses, but with daily wear, daily rubbing, and the occasional drop onto a table, fine scratches build up on the surface, the edge can develop small chips, and the curvature can slowly change. Scratches trap deposits, so the lens gets hazier however carefully you clean it. Once the curvature changes, the way the lens sits on your cornea changes with it: it moves more than before, and the foreign-body sensation comes back. These changes happen so gradually that daily wearers rarely notice them, and often assume their eyes have just become more sensitive.

That's why, at follow-up visits, our optometry staff don't only check how the lenses fit on your eyes; they also take the lenses out and inspect them. Whether and when to replace them has no fixed answer in years; it depends on the condition of your two lenses right now. Good care makes lenses last longer, and lenses that have lasted a long time need someone to take a regular look at them.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I rinse RGP lenses with tap water?**

No. Tap water and bottled water aren't sterile. Rinsing a lens in untreated water and then putting it on your eye means placing whatever microorganisms were in the water onto your cornea too. Always rinse and soak with the solution named in the instructions, and the same goes for the case: don't wash it with tap water. When you wash your face or shower with lenses in, try not to let water get directly into your eyes.

**Q: What if I drop my RGP lens and can't find it?**

RGP lenses are small and rigid, so they usually come through a fall intact. Don't move your feet at first; look down and check your clothing and the floor around you. Once you find it, clean and disinfect it fully before wearing it again. If the lens has a chip or a crack, don't wear it; bring it in for us to check.

**Q: My eye hurts when sand gets in while wearing RGP lenses. Is that normal?**

Yes. A foreign object getting between the lens and the cornea is more noticeable with RGP than with soft lenses, because of the lens's small diameter and stable shape. The right response is to take the lens out right away, rinse it, and put it back in. If pain or redness continues after that, stop wearing it and see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible. Wind protection in dusty or sandy conditions reduces how often this happens.

**Q: I clean my lenses carefully, so why do they still look hazy?**

Three common causes: not doing protein removal often enough, reusing solution, or fine scratches already on the lens surface. First check whether your daily cleaning and protein-removal schedule matches the product instructions; if the haze persists after adjusting that, bring the lenses to your follow-up for our optometry staff to check their condition.
