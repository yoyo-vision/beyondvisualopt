## A 10-Minute Eye Exam vs. a 1-Hour Eye Exam: The Difference Isn't "Fast or Slow" — It's "What Got Done"

Ten minutes versus an hour — which one is more professional?

That's actually the wrong question. **The length of time is a result, not the goal.** Some exams wrap up in ten minutes, others take close to an hour. The real difference isn't who's being more meticulous and slow — it's how many extra steps happened in that time, and what question each step is answering about your eyes.

A process where you sit down, read an eye chart, pick whichever looks clearer, and swap frames is measuring "a prescription that lets you see clearly." A full eye exam, on top of the prescription, also accounts for how you use your eyes, how your two eyes work together, and exactly where these glasses will sit on your face. Working through those steps naturally takes longer — not because anyone's dragging it out, but because every step has a reason it needs to happen.

This article isn't saying a quick exam is always bad — a short session can absolutely handle a simple prescription tweak. What we want to do is lay out what actually happens during that "hour," so you can judge for yourself how far your own situation needs to go.

### What Question Each Step Is Answering

| Step | The Question This Step Answers |
|---|---|
| Consultation: understanding how you use your eyes | Are you mostly looking far or near each day? What does this pair of glasses need to solve? |
| Old-glasses analysis (lensmeter reading) | What's your current prescription, and how far off is it from your current eyes? |
| Auto-refraction for a starting value | Roughly what range is your prescription in? |
| Objective/subjective cross-check | Does what the machine measured match what you actually see? |
| Binocular vision function assessment | Do your two eyes coordinate smoothly when working together? |
| Near-vision add assessment (age-dependent) | How much correction do you need for comfortable near work? |
| Trial fitting and fine-tuning | Wearing the prescription you'll actually use, does it feel clear and comfortable? |
| Dispensing measurements | How does this prescription get precisely fitted into the frame you chose? |

## Breaking It Down: Where the Extra Time Actually Goes

**Consultation on how you use your eyes.** This step uses no equipment, yet it's often the most critical one. An engineer staring at a screen for over 10 hours a day, a salesperson who does night drives, and an accountant who spends most of the day reading spreadsheets — even with the exact same prescription, the best lens design and prescription strategy for each of them is completely different. Without first understanding how you actually use your eyes, no matter how accurately everything gets measured afterward, you might still end up with glasses that "score perfectly on the eye chart but feel wrong in real life."

**Old-glasses analysis.** Putting your current glasses on a lensmeter immediately shows how much your prescription has changed over the years, and whether your old pair was under- or over-corrected. This is an important reference for deciding how the new prescription should be adjusted, and whether to jump straight to the full target value. Skipping it is like showing up without half your medical history.

**Objective/subjective cross-check.** Auto-refraction gives you a "starting value" — a starting point, not the final answer. Next comes confirming, back and forth with an eye chart and actual trial lenses, "is this clearer, or is that clearer," matching the machine's objective data against your subjective experience. This back-and-forth is where most of the time goes, and it's the key to getting an accurate prescription.

**Binocular vision function.** Your eyes work as a pair. Each eye seeing clearly on its own doesn't mean the two eyes work together effortlessly — eye alignment, convergence, and fusion can only be measured when both eyes are working at the same time. A quick process usually skips this step entirely, even though it has an outsized effect on how comfortable your eyes feel over long use. To understand how the two eyes coordinate, see: [What Is "Binocular Vision"? How Your Two Eyes Work Together](/en/blog/binocular-vision/).

**Trial fitting, fine-tuning, and dispensing measurements.** Once the prescription is set, you wear the actual prescription you'll be using, walk around, and switch between distance and near to confirm it's not just "clear standing still at an eye chart" but comfortable in everyday situations too. Then we measure pupillary distance, pupil height, vertex distance, and other fitting parameters to precisely translate that prescription into the frame you chose.

These steps actually map onto most of the common pitfalls behind "I got glasses and they still don't feel right." We've laid out which step getting skipped leads to which problem in this article: [5 Common Mistakes in Eye Exams When Getting Glasses](/en/blog/common-optometry-mistakes/).

## One More Distinction: The Eye Exam and Dispensing Are Two Separate Things

A lot of people lump "eye exam" and "dispensing" together, but they're two separate things: the eye exam measures your prescription and parameters, and dispensing turns that prescription into a physical pair of glasses — both stages affect how the glasses feel once you're wearing them. We've broken down that distinction separately here: [What's the Difference Between an Eye Exam and Dispensing?](/en/blog/optometry-vs-dispensing/)

Two compliance notes worth adding: first, if during the process we find that vision just won't correct up to a reasonable level, or notice something that looks unusual, we'll refer you to an ophthalmologist under Taiwan's Optometrists Act rather than pushing ahead with dispensing. Second, some procedures (such as cycloplegic refraction, which fully relaxes the ciliary muscle with dilating drops) are medical procedures — please see an ophthalmologist for those. Refraction for schoolchildren age 6 to 15 must by law be carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance, and children under 6 are not permitted by law to be refracted by an optometrist — please have them evaluated by an ophthalmologist. At the end of the day, a good optometrist doesn't just know how to measure — they know when to point you toward a physician. [A Good Optometrist Matters More Than a Good Lens](/en/guide/why-optometrist-matters/) is exactly about that kind of judgment.

## FAQ

**Q1: Does a longer eye exam mean it's more professional?**

Not necessarily. Time is a natural result of "getting through the steps that need doing" — it's not something to show off. A simple prescription tweak can be handled quickly, but if you have fatigue from long-term use, adaptation issues with progressive lenses, or concerns about binocular coordination, then working through consultation, binocular vision testing, and trial fitting will naturally take longer. What matters is always "what got done," not the number on a stopwatch.

**Q2: My prescription only changed a little — does it still take an hour?**

Not necessarily. If your needs are simple, your eyes are otherwise fine, and you just want a minor prescription adjustment, the process can be focused and streamlined. The value of a full eye exam is in helping you find the reason when you "can't quite explain why something feels off," working through it step by step. How deep it needs to go is adjusted to your situation — not everyone needs the full package.

**Q3: What does a full eye exam actually include?**

Roughly: a consultation about how you use your eyes, old-glasses analysis, auto-refraction for a starting value, an objective/subjective cross-check, binocular vision function assessment, near-vision add (age-dependent), trial fitting and fine-tuning, and dispensing measurements. Each item answers a question — how you use your eyes, what your prescription is, whether your two eyes coordinate, whether your near vision is sufficient, whether it feels comfortable to wear, and how it gets fitted into the frame.

**Q4: Do children need this full a process too?**

A child's vision is still developing, so the items that need assessing aren't fewer. One thing to keep in mind: under Taiwan's Optometrists Act, refraction for schoolchildren under 15 must be carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance; children under 6 should see an ophthalmologist first for a medical evaluation. Within that framework, we coordinate with ophthalmologists on vision assessment and prescription tracking.
