## Same Prescription, Still Worlds Apart

Those two pairs of glasses in your hands — the prescription numbers (myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism) copied down are identical, yet one feels fine and the other makes you dizzy.

That's not strange at all. **How comfortable a pair of glasses feels depends on prescription for only one variable out of many.** There are another six or seven parameters at play: whether the astigmatism axis is right, whether the optical center aligns with your pupil, how far the lens sits from your eye, how many degrees the frame tilts forward. Each one is quietly rewriting "what this prescription actually produces once it's on your face."

Here's an analogy: the numbers on your prescription are like "5 grams of salt" in a recipe. The same 5 grams of salt tastes completely different depending on heat, cooking order, and pan size. Glasses work the same way — getting the prescription right is just the passing grade. What actually determines how it feels is whether the entire set of fitting parameters beyond the prescription has been matched to you.

### 30-Second Read: Parameters Beyond Prescription That Also Determine Comfort

| Parameter | What it controls | What happens if it's off |
|---|---|---|
| Astigmatism axis | The angle astigmatism correction needs to "rotate" to | A few degrees off, and vision gets blurry with a tugging feeling |
| Pupillary distance (PD) | Aligning the lens's optical center with your pupil | An off-center alignment creates a prismatic effect, straining your eyes |
| Fitting height | Where prescription meets your line of sight vertically on the lens | Especially critical for progressives — the clear zone shifts entirely |
| Vertex distance | How far the lens sits from your cornea | Changes the effective power you actually experience at high prescriptions |
| Pantoscopic tilt | The forward tilt angle of the frame | Affects astigmatism correction and the lower field of view |
| Base curve | The curvature of the lens itself | A big mismatch in curve makes the periphery feel off |
| Frame fit | Whether the frame sits stable and level on your face | Constant slipping or unevenness undoes all the careful measurement |

## What's Actually Going On With Each of These Variables

**Astigmatism axis: a few degrees makes a real difference.** Astigmatism isn't just a number — it also has a direction, and axis is that direction. The same -1.00 astigmatism at a 90-degree axis versus a 100-degree axis are two different things; if the correction angle isn't rotated right, straight lines look slightly tilted and text has an indescribable blur to it. If one pair of glasses has the axis nailed and the other is off by ten-plus degrees, the prescription line can look identical while the actual experience is worlds apart.

**Pupillary distance and fitting height: where the prescription actually lands.** Every lens has an optical center — the single point where its optical performance is cleanest — and it needs to line up with your pupil during fitting. Left-right alignment depends on pupillary distance (PD); up-down placement depends on fitting height. Miss the alignment, and your line of sight passes through the lens's edge instead, creating a prismatic effect that forces your brain to work harder to compensate. When people say "my new glasses just feel off somehow," this is often exactly where the problem lies, optically speaking. For more detail, see [What Is Pupillary Distance (PD)? What Happens If It's Measured Wrong](/en/blog/pupillary-distance-pd/).

**Vertex distance: how far the lens sits from your eye.** A lens sits in front of your nose bridge with a small gap between it and your eyeball, and that gap affects the effective power you actually experience — the higher your prescription, the more noticeable the effect. The same prescription fitted into two frames with different vertex distances can produce different effective powers. Details here: [What Is "Vertex Distance"? Why It Matters for High Prescriptions](/en/blog/vertex-distance/).

**Pantoscopic tilt, base curve, and frame fit: easy to overlook, but very noticeable.** How many degrees the frame tilts forward, how curved the lens itself is, and whether the frame sits level and stable on your face — get any of these wrong, and even the most precise measurements upstream get undermined. A frame that keeps sliding down, or sits higher on one side, means you're seeing the world every day through parameters that have already drifted out of position.

## A Real Scenario: 2mm for a Progressive Lens Wearer

Here's a situation we see often in the shop. An office worker who stares at a screen more than 10 hours a day, and who's just started needing progressives, had two pairs of glasses with identical prescription and add power — but the fitting height on one was measured accurately, and the other was off by 2mm. **For progressives, a 2mm difference in fitting height means the entire near-vision clear zone shifts up or down by that much.** When he looked down at his screen, his line of sight landed on the transition zone instead of the near zone — so even though the prescription was correct, he kept having to hunt for the right angle to look through, and got noticeably more tired by afternoon. That wasn't a prescription problem. It was those 2mm not lining up with the exact spot he was actually using every day.

## FAQ

**Q1: Same prescription, but two pairs of glasses feel really different — does that mean one of them was fitted wrong?**

Not necessarily. Identical prescriptions only mean the "prescription line" matches — but astigmatism axis, PD, fitting height, vertex distance, pantoscopic tilt, and frame fit can all differ, and that alone changes how each pair feels. Rather than guessing which one was fitted wrong, bring both in and let an optometrist measure both sets of parameters — that's how you find out where the comfortable one wins and where the uncomfortable one falls short.

**Q2: I got a new pair made with my old prescription, so why doesn't it feel right?**

Because "copying" the prescription only carries over the numbers — not the entire set of fitting parameters that came with your old glasses. A new frame's size, shape, and position on your face are all different, which changes fitting height, vertex distance, and pantoscopic tilt — so the same prescription in a new frame naturally feels different. That's why the fitting measurements genuinely can't be skipped when getting a new pair made.

**Q3: Do these parameters matter more the higher the prescription?**

Yes. Errors in vertex distance and pupillary distance both get amplified as prescription increases. At a low prescription, a slight inaccuracy might go unnoticed; at a high prescription, the same margin of error becomes immediately noticeable. That's why anyone with a high prescription should be especially attentive to fitting precision, and should specifically seek out an optometrist with a thorough measurement process.

**Q4: Can I measure these parameters myself?**

We wouldn't recommend it. Pupillary distance, fitting height, vertex distance, and pantoscopic tilt all need to be measured with the frame selected and worn in its normal position, by an optometrist observing your actual fit — that's the only way to capture how you really wear your glasses. What you can do is simply wear the frame in the position you normally would, so the measurement reflects your everyday use.
