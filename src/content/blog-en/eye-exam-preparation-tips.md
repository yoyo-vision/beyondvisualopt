## A Good Eye Exam Is Half Expertise, Half How Clearly You Tell Us

An eye exam isn't something you sit through passively while being "checked." **However carefully the optometry staff measure, they can't know what you haven't told them** — what distance you actually spend your day looking at, which situation bothers you most, what it was about your last pair of glasses that you couldn't stand. Only you know those things. The more clearly you describe them, the more the exam shifts from "guessing an average" to "aimed at your life."

Spend five minutes preparing, and your exam will go noticeably better.

## Pre-Exam Checklist

| Category | What to prepare |
|---|---|
| Bring | The glasses you wear now (including your spare pair and the pair you find uncomfortable), and the box from your contact lenses that shows the power |
| Think through | Your main viewing distances and hours each day, which situation you most want fixed, what was uncomfortable about your last pair |
| Mention | Your job and how you use your eyes, whether you drive (especially at night), how far away your screen is, any dizziness or sore eyes |
| Contact lens wearers | How you've worn them on the day of the exam — ideally take them out early so your corneas can rest for a while |
| Please tell us | Chronic conditions, recent eye treatment, and any eye drops you're currently using |

## What to Bring: Your Old Glasses and Your Contact Lens Box

**Bring the glasses you wear now, especially the pair that "doesn't feel right."** Once the optometry staff put them on the lensmeter, they can see your current power straight away, how far it is from where your eyes are now, and whether your last pair was under- or over-corrected. It saves time, and it's far more accurate than starting from zero. Even the pair you're fed up with and ready to throw out is worth bringing, because "what's wrong with it" is a clue in itself.

**Contact lens wearers, bring the box as well.** Contact lens and glasses powers can't simply be copied from one to the other (the vertex distance is different — a lens sitting on the cornea and a lens resting on your nose a little in front of the eye need different powers), but the contact lens power you wear now is still a good reference point to start from.

## Tell the Optometry Staff How You Actually Use Your Eyes

This is the part most people overlook, and it's the part that most affects which direction your glasses should go. With exactly the same prescription, the best lens design for someone who stares at a screen all day and for someone who's always out driving to see clients can be completely different. So please be specific:

- **Screen distance and hours**: How many hours a day do you look at screens? Roughly how far is the screen from your eyes? (An arm's length? Closer?)
- **Driving**: Do you drive often, and do you drive at night? At night, do oncoming headlights feel glaring or show halos?
- **Reading and close work**: Do you do a lot of close-up things — reading, looking at your phone, handicrafts?
- **The one thing you most want fixed**: If these glasses could only improve one situation, which would it be?

The more specific, the better. "My eyes get especially tired on the computer in the afternoon, and the text goes a bit blurry" is a hundred times more useful than "my eyes don't feel great." This is the first step of a full eye exam: questions first, then measurements, so the prescription fits your life. ## How to Describe Eye Discomfort to the Optometry Staff

"Uncomfortable" on its own is hard to act on. Try breaking it down from these angles instead:

- **When**: Fine in the morning, then bothering you by afternoon? Only when looking up close, or at a distance too?
- **What it feels like**: Blurry, dizzy, a feeling of pressure, sore, seeing double, or like something is pulling?
- **In which situation**: Looking at a screen, reading, driving, or walking down stairs?
- **Does covering one eye help**: This is an important clue about how well your two eyes work together.

That last one is especially useful. If you find that "covering one eye is actually more comfortable than using both," it may involve how your two eyes coordinate, not just the power of each eye. A full eye exam specifically assesses binocular vision to catch exactly this kind of situation. ## Things to Tell Us Before the Exam

- **Contact lens wearers**: For soft contact lenses, we suggest taking them out early before the exam so your corneas can rest for a while; rigid lenses need longer. Please also tell us how you've worn them that day, so the measurements are accurate. One more point: general contact lens fitting is regulated under Taiwan's Optometrists Act — for those aged 15 or under it must be done under an ophthalmologist's guidance, and it may not be done for children under 6. Our stores currently do not fit contact lenses for anyone aged 15 or under.
- **Chronic conditions and medication**: Please tell us about chronic conditions such as diabetes, any eye drops you're using, and any recent eye treatment or surgery. These affect our judgment and whether we refer you on.
- **Warning signs — say so straight away**: If your vision has recently dropped suddenly, you're seeing dark shadows or flashes, or your eye is red, swollen or painful, that's not something glasses can deal with. Please see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible rather than waiting for an eye exam.
- **Children's eye exams**: Under Taiwan's Optometrists Act, optometry staff may not perform refraction for children under 6 — please take them to an ophthalmologist. For ages 6 to 15 (inclusive), before the first refraction and the first pair of glasses, a physician must confirm the myopia is not pseudomyopia; all refraction in this age group is carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance. Children over 15 can have a refraction directly. Before bringing your child in, think about how they manage at distance and near day to day, where they sit at school, and how much time they spend on screens — all of this is useful information.

With this preparation done, you'll find the eye exam becomes a conversation rather than a check you passively undergo. ## FAQ

**Q: Do I have to bring my old glasses to an eye exam? What happens if I don't?**

We strongly recommend it, especially the pair you find uncomfortable. Your old glasses are a ready-made reference for your prescription: one check shows your current power, how much it has changed, and what was off about your last pair. Not bringing them doesn't mean you can't be examined, but the optometry staff lose an important reference and have to spend more time working things out from scratch. Bring the box or packaging from your contact lenses that shows the power, too.

**Q: Do I need to rest my eyes before an eye exam?**

It helps. Avoiding long stretches of close-up work before the exam and getting enough sleep reduces the chance of an over-tensed ciliary muscle making your prescription measure stronger than it is. Contact lens wearers should take their lenses out early so the corneas can recover (soft lenses for a while, rigid lenses longer), and please tell us how you wore them that day.

**Q: I don't know how to describe my discomfort. What should I do?**

Just break it down into "when, what it feels like, and in which situation." For example: "In the afternoon on the computer, the text goes blurry and there's a bit of pressure." Add "does covering one eye feel more comfortable," and that's already a valuable clue. You don't need to know any technical terms; describe what you feel day to day in specific words and leave the translating to the optometry staff.

**Q: What should I prepare before bringing my child for an eye exam?**

Think about how your child manages at distance and near day to day, how far from the board they sit at school, how long they spend on screens each day, and whether they squint or lean in close to see. These everyday observations help a lot with the assessment. Please also note: under Taiwan's Optometrists Act, optometry staff may not perform refraction for children under 6 — please take them to an ophthalmologist. For ages 6 to 15 (inclusive), before the first refraction and the first pair of glasses, a physician must confirm the myopia is not pseudomyopia; all refraction in this age group is carried out under an ophthalmologist's guidance, and we follow up on your child's prescription within that division of roles, in coordination with the physician. Children over 15 can have a refraction directly.
