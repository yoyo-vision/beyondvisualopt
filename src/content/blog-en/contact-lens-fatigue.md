## "Fine in the morning, but by four o'clock I want them out"

We hear this often in store. The people saying it have usually worn soft contact lenses for years. Putting them in in the morning is fine, but by the afternoon their eyes are dry and gritty, text on the screen starts to blur, and lately they can barely make it to the evening. Some assume their eyes have got worse; some try a different lens, with much the same result.

Contact lens fatigue rarely has a single cause. The lens, the hours you wear it, your surroundings, your prescription, and your eyes themselves often combine. The first few can be adjusted through habits and fitting; the condition of your eyes themselves needs an ophthalmologist.

---

## 5 common reasons contact lenses get tiring

### The lens draws water from your tears, more so in air-conditioning and at a screen

Soft lenses get their softness from a water-containing material, and they draw water from the tear film on the surface of your eye to keep their own water content up. In an air-conditioned office, or after long hours at a screen, tears evaporate faster, and you blink less while staring at a screen; each blink is what spreads the tear film evenly again. The drier your eyes get, the more the lens pulls water from your tears, and the drier the surface becomes. Afternoon discomfort usually builds up this way.

### Long hours leave the cornea short of oxygen

The cornea has no blood vessels and gets its oxygen mainly from the air through the tear film. With a lens on top, how much gets through depends on the lens material and thickness, and the longer you wear it, the more strain it puts on the cornea. A typical pattern is fine in the morning, uncomfortable by the afternoon, and eyes that redden easily. Plenty of people put their lenses in at 7 a.m. and take them out at 11 p.m., which is sixteen hours. Over the long term, oxygen shortage can cause new blood vessels to grow into the edge of the cornea (American Academy of Ophthalmology EyeWiki, "Contact Lens Complications"); whether that's happening, and how serious it is, needs to be checked by an ophthalmologist.

### Deposits on the lens

The surface of a soft lens readily picks up proteins and lipids from your tears, particles from the air, and residue from makeup and sunscreen. With two-week or monthly lenses worn day after day, this keeps building up: vision turns hazy, and your eyes feel gritty or as if something is in them. Daily disposables are new every day, so nothing builds up; with reusable lenses, the replacement period counts from the day you open the pack, not from how many times you've worn them.

### The prescription hasn't kept up with how you use your eyes

Contact lens prescriptions are usually set for the clearest distance vision. If you spend long hours at close work, a power that's just right for distance means your eyes keep working hard up close, and a myopic prescription on the strong side makes near work even more tiring. Around forty, accommodation starts to decline, and contact lens wearers notice it earlier than glasses wearers do; why is explained in the last section of [Contact Lenses or Glasses?](/en/blog/contact-lens-vs-glasses/).

### A big difference between the two eyes

When the two eyes have very different prescriptions, the work of aiming and focusing them together is already heavier, and they tire more easily over long stretches. Contact lenses have advantages in this situation and also things to watch for, covered in [the guide to large differences between the two eyes](/en/guide/aniseikonia/).

## Five Things to Do First When Your Contacts Feel Uncomfortable

**1. Check your lens material**
Soft lenses come in more than one material, with different water content and oxygen permeability. Which one suits you depends on how many hours a day you wear lenses, where you spend most of your time, and whether they feel dry by the afternoon; our optometry staff go through these with you at the fitting. Don't just switch to a different lens on your own.

**2. Wear them a few hours less each day**
How long you can wear lenses each day follows the advice our optometry staff give you and the product instructions. If you regularly go past that, start here.

**3. Switch to glasses when you get home**
Once you're home for the evening, take the lenses out and put your glasses on, so your eyes get a few hours without a lens covering them. Your glasses need an adequate prescription for this to work.

**4. Replace on schedule, clean as the instructions say**
Replace reusable lenses when they're due, change the solution every day, and replace the case regularly; daily disposables are worn once and thrown away, never kept for the next day.

**5. If you've worn lenses for years, have an ophthalmologist check your eye surface regularly**
After years of contact lens wear, the condition of your cornea and tear film should be checked regularly by an ophthalmologist, who decides how often. Our stores don't perform these two checks.

## Dry, red, gritty: take them out first, and for these, go straight to an eye doctor

If your eyes feel dry, red, or gritty while wearing lenses, take the lenses out and rest your eyes. If they're still dry after resting, please have an ophthalmologist check them; whether to use eye drops, and which kind, is the doctor's decision.

Redness, pain, light sensitivity, tearing that won't stop, a sudden drop in vision, or more discharge than usual is no longer a comfort issue: stop wearing the lenses and see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible, and bring the lenses and the case. How to judge the urgency of these symptoms is covered in [Eye Doctor or Optometry Clinic? The Symptom Triage Guide](/en/guide/eye-doctor-or-optometrist/).

## Bought the same lenses for years? Your prescription may be years old too

The most convenient thing about contact lenses is that you can keep buying the same ones. That's also where the trouble starts: many people's contact lens prescription was measured years ago, and every time since, they've simply bought another box with the same numbers.

Over those years your prescription may have shifted, and the distances you look at every day have changed too: a new job, one screen turned into three, more time on your phone than before. Around forty, near-focusing ability starts to decline as well. The lenses are still the same box, and they go from clear to just about adequate, then from adequate to tiring by the afternoon. Because it happens gradually, few people think to go back and check the prescription; they blame the lenses or the air-conditioning first.

The American Optometric Association's 2023 adult guideline recommends a comprehensive eye and vision examination every year for adults, and its 2015 risk-factor list includes contact lens wear. If your contacts are tiring by the afternoon, before you rush to try a different lens, check what year your prescription is from.

## FAQ

**Q: How long can I wear contact lenses before my eyes get dry?**

There's no fixed time; it depends on your tear production, the lens material, and your surroundings. Air-conditioning, screen work, and longer wearing hours all make dryness arrive sooner. If you feel uncomfortable by the afternoon every day, try the five steps above for a while; if nothing changes, come back for a fresh eye exam and a check on how you're wearing your lenses, and if dryness persists, have an ophthalmologist examine your eyes.

**Q: Can I occasionally sleep in my contact lenses overnight?**

We strongly advise against it. With your eyes closed during sleep, your cornea already gets less oxygen than when your eyes are open, and the lens is covering it on top of that. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's EyeWiki entry "Contact Lens Complications" lists overnight wear as one of the primary risk factors for corneal infection. You may feel nothing in the short term, but an infection can affect your vision, so even "just once in a while" isn't worth it.

**Q: My eyes feel dry with contacts. Can I just buy eye drops myself?**

Don't buy them on your own yet. There are many kinds of eye drops, not all of them can be used with lenses in, and the dryness may not be coming from the lens. If dryness persists, please have an ophthalmologist examine your eyes; whether to use drops, which kind, and whether they can go in with lenses in is the doctor's decision. If it's only occasional dryness after long wear, take the lenses out to rest your eyes, and look back at how many hours you've worn them and where you've been these past few days.

Contact lens fatigue rarely has a single cause. Lens material, wearing hours, the state of your tear film, office air-conditioning and screens usually pile up together and catch up with you around four in the afternoon. That's why "trying a different lens" doesn't always help; first you need to know where the problem actually lies.
